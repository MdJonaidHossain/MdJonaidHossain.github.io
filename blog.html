Four years of undergrad, in the order they actually happen

I did my BSc in Bangladesh and then moved through six countries for the next degrees. Most of what mattered was sequencing — doing the right thing in the right year. Nobody told me the order, so here it is.

Year one

Learn the place, then learn the subject

A new city is a skill before it is a backdrop. Walk it until you can move without a map, learn the transit lines, and write down where the nearest hospital, pharmacy and police station are. Keep two local phone numbers you can call at 3 a.m.

  • Join one society that touches the career you think you want, and one purely for the people.
  • Go to the lectures. Attendance is the cheapest grade you will ever buy.
  • Take notes by hand and reread them the same week — the rereading is the part that works.
Year two

Get into a lab

By now a few lecturers know your face. That is the whole asset. Ask them directly whether they need a pair of hands — most research assistantships are given, not advertised.

  • Expect to be turned down several times. The yes you need is the fourth or fifth one.
  • Go to a conference or a summer school, even online. Two days of it rewires how you see the field.
  • Learn to dress plainly and well. First impressions are shorter in science than people admit.
Year three

Leave the country, and sit the exams

An exchange programme is the cheapest way to find out how differently a laboratory can be run. Choose the programme before the destination, start the visa early, and budget twice the time you think the paperwork needs.

  • Book GRE, TOEFL or IELTS for early in year four and prepare with the official material, not the shortcuts.
  • A score that is good enough, early, beats a perfect score that arrives after the deadline.
Year four

Publish something, and apply everywhere

Whatever you did in the lab in year two or three can usually become a paper — ask your supervisor what shape it should take. A published undergraduate is a different applicant from an unpublished one.

  • Start scholarship applications in the first semester. The documents take longer than the writing.
  • A statement of purpose is an argument, not a biography: what you want to work on, and why you specifically.
  • Ask for recommendation letters early, and give each referee your CV and a one-paragraph reminder of what you did for them.

And then stop, for a month or two. The degree is four years of your life and you only get to finish it once — spend the last of it with the people you did it alongside before the work starts.
